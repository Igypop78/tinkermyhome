A colleague got me into solar in 2017. He showed me that you don't need much to make it work: a grid-tie inverter, a few panels and a wall socket. Since then I have rebuilt my system more times than I'd like to admit. Here is the whole journey – mistakes included.

What I have today is summed up in [My solar setup](/en/energy/my-solar/). This article is about how I got there.

## Timeline

| When | What was added |
|---|---|
| **2017** | Sun 1 kW grid-tie inverter, 4 used 250 W panels, official grid connection (1 kW export limit) |
| **then** | Lead-acid battery 2 × 160 Ah with an MPPT charge controller – gone after six months |
| **then** | Second Sun 2 kW inverter and stronger used panels, export still max. 1 kW |
| **~2021** | Panels on the house roof, DIY frame from L-profiles, GoodWe 5 kW hybrid, U/f guard, first LiFePO4 105 Ah |
| **2024** | Deye 10 kW inverter, more panels, 10 kW export limit, 280 Ah battery |
| **2024** | Second 280 Ah battery in a case → 34 kWh in total. In April, frameless DAH panels on the carport |
| **spring 2026** | Everything from scratch: K2 mounting system, 22 × JA Solar 500 W, repaired roof |

## 2017: an inverter in a wall socket

The first one was a small **Sun 1 kW grid-tie inverter**. Back then it cost **€400**. I paired it with **4 used 250 W panels** – huge ones, **€100 each**. That's all you need to start: the panels produce DC, the inverter turns it into AC and feeds it into the house wiring.

![My first used 250 W panel, the Sun inverter with its display and the Epever MPPT controller from the lead-acid days](/img/clanky/fve-cesta-zaciatok.jpg)

The tech was simple, the paperwork wasn't. Making it legal meant **design documentation, certificates, declarations, an inspection report** and a pile of other things. All by e-mail, and it took **about 4 months**. But it worked out – I was officially connected with a **1 kW export limit**. By the way, I still have that inverter on a shelf at home.

:::warn Plug-in solar is not a toy
Even a small grid-tie inverter has to be registered with the grid operator and connected by the rules. An unregistered source on the grid is illegal and can be dangerous when something fails – for example for an electrician working on a line that is supposed to be dead.
:::

## Lead-acid: a step sideways

I immediately wanted to store energy for later too. LiFePO4 was unknown and expensive to me back then, so I went for **lead-acid – 2 × 160 Ah**. Charging was handled by an **Epever Tracer MPPT controller** and the inverter drew power straight from the battery. The Sun inverter had a 22–65 V input, so a **24 V setup** was enough to start with.

After six months I found out I could reasonably use **only half of the capacity**. So I got rid of it and started **shifting consumption to the hours of production** instead – running appliances while the sun shines.

:::tip Why only half
You shouldn't discharge a lead-acid battery below ~50 %, otherwise it wears out quickly. Out of 160 Ah you really get around 80 Ah. LiFePO4 easily handles 80–90 % of its capacity and thousands of cycles.
:::

## A second inverter and solar fever

Seeing it work got me so excited that I soon added **another Sun 2 kW inverter** and stronger panels – second-hand again. Export stayed limited to **1 kW**.

The fever kept rising and the unused house roof kept me awake at night. Until then, everything was on the carport only. More panels came, a **DIY frame from L-profiles, holes in the roof** and a pile of quick makeshift solutions. I thought I'd save money – original flat-roof mounting systems cost more than the panels.

Plus a **GoodWe GW5048-EM hybrid inverter from a classifieds site**, in parallel with the original Sun. Used **OMSUN 260 W** panels were added as well.

![GoodWe 5 kW hybrid with the Sun on the wall and the distribution box with the U/f guard; on the right a Gobel LiFePO4 cell (EVE LF280K)](/img/clanky/fve-cesta-goodwe.jpg)

Since I want everything done properly, I went through the whole process with the grid operator **again**. With two inverters I also had to install a **U/f guard** – a protection relay that disconnects the sources when grid voltage or frequency is off. It wasn't cheap, but above all it was impossible to find anywhere.

## My first LiFePO4 battery

Along the way I built my **first LiFePO4 battery**: **105 Ah** cells and a **JK BMS 200 A**. I learned as I went – compression or not, which cables, which fuses. And above all, how to set up the inverter and the BMS so they talk to each other. It worked. This DIY pack still runs today.

## A steep tilt and restless nights

For the frame I went for the **optimal tilt** to get the most out of the panels – especially outside summer. That meant a steep angle and a **large surface catching the wind**. It survived on the roof for a while, but I didn't sleep well. So I climbed back up and **lowered the tilt** so the panels wouldn't act like a sail.

![The old DIY frame with a lower tilt on the flat roof](/img/clanky/fve-cesta-stara-konstrukcia.jpg)

## Deye and the 10 kW ceiling

In 2024 the roof still wasn't full, panel prices were falling like autumn leaves, and a **Deye inverter** arrived on the market that met all my needs. Another round: new inverter, more panels, a bigger frame and new paperwork. This time **online and fast**. The result: a **10 kW export limit** – the ceiling I can feed into the grid as a private person.

The inverter got **280 Ah cells from Gobel Power**, ordered directly from China. I assembled them on a shelf with a BMS. Over time it started to bother me – the battery **wasn't compressed** and the cells began to swell slightly (no, they weren't overcharged).

![Batteries on the shelf next to the Deye – no case, no compression](/img/clanky/fve-cesta-bateria-polica.jpg)

So I decided the next battery would be done **properly**: more 280 Ah cells, **two cases** with a proper BMS and fusing. The first 280 Ah cells moved into a case too. Only the 105 Ah pack stayed outside. Today that's my **~34 kWh**.

![280 Ah cells assembled before moving into a case, and today's state – Deye, two Gobel cases and the 105 Ah pack in a box](/img/clanky/fve-cesta-bateria-skrine.jpg)

The system was fully working again, I just struggled with the communication and accuracy of the JK BMS. That's a chapter of its own – I wrote it up in [Accurate SOC in a Deye inverter](/en/energy/soc-from-smartshunt-to-deye/).

## 2024: new panels on the carport

In April 2024 I replaced the old panels on the carport with **6 frameless DAH panels** (~480 Wp). The camera recorded it – the swap took just under **an hour and a half**.

![The carport before and after: old panels and the frameless DAH ones](/img/clanky/fve-cesta-dah-vymena.jpg)

## Once and properly

The roof was almost full and the total was only about **8.5 kWp**. On top of that I still wasn't happy with the homemade frame **drilled through the roof membrane into the beams**. Yes, you read that right – I turned my roof into a sieve. Nothing ever leaked, but I wasn't at peace with it. And the **electric car** pushed up demand for energy.

So came – from today's point of view – the final decision: **let's do it once and do it properly**. A proper frame, proper panels that use as much of the area as possible, built to last "forever".

After a month of planning and research: **K2 flat-roof mounting system, JA Solar 500 W panels** and above all a plan for **patching the roof**. I learned to **weld Fatrafol PVC membrane** and in spring 2026 off we went. Panels down, old frame removed, roof cleaned, holes repaired and the new setup installed.

![Removal: the old frames without panels](/img/clanky/fve-cesta-demontaz.jpg)

![Installation: K2 rails and new JA Solar panels ready on the roof](/img/clanky/fve-cesta-k2-montaz.jpg)

![Done: 22 JA Solar 500 W panels on the K2 system](/img/clanky/fve-cesta-hotovo.jpg)

Today the roof holds **22 × JA Solar 500 W** and the carport 6 × DAH – about **13.9 kWp** in total. The system runs flawlessly. Not that a few more panels wouldn't earn their keep…

## What it cost

I probably have the most expensive solar system in the neighbourhood if I count all the previous rounds – and I'd rather not know. The current setup:

| Part | Price |
|---|---|
| Deye 10 kW inverter | €1,700 |
| Panels (incl. shipping) | €1,700 |
| Battery ~34 kWh | €3,500 |
| K2 mounting system | ~€1,000 |
| Cables and the rest | ~€300 (estimate) |
| **Total** | **~€8,200** |
| Labour | priceless 🙂 |

:::warn The price assumes DIY installation
I mounted and wired everything **myself**, and the finished installation was checked by an **external electrical inspector**. The inspection is mandatory – without the report, the grid operator won't connect your system. The price doesn't include an installer's labour. If you have no experience with electrics, I don't recommend doing it yourself.
:::

## What I learned

1. **It is highly addictive.** Making your own energy and being at least a bit independent of the grid – once it gets you, it won't let go.
2. **Do things properly the first time**, even if it looks more expensive. Trust me, compromise solutions are the most expensive ones.
3. **Go for the maximum** your roof and grid operator allow, even if you don't seem to need it today. The demand will come – an electric car, water heating, cooling…
4. **But despite points 1–3**, starting small is better than not starting at all. 🙂

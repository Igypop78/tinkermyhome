I won't lie, I liked electric cars from the start. Not because they're "green", but because the principle is **far simpler** than a combustion engine. And because I can charge the car from my own solar panels – which gives me a sense of independence.

So what's it like in reality?

## It started with a plug-in hybrid

My road to an EV began with a **Ford Kuga plug-in hybrid** – lightly used, imported from Germany. The silence and instant throttle response won me over right away. Great for everyday driving. And the 2.5-litre engine had no trouble even with a flat battery.

On longer trips, though, I started wondering what a pure EV would be like. Even with the plug-in we stopped more often than refuelling required. **The 700 km range wasn't the limit – the limit was how long we were willing to sit in the car without a break.**

## Tesla Model Y RWD

![Our Tesla Model Y – turns out the frunk isn’t just for luggage 🙂](/img/clanky/elektromobil-tesla.jpg)

It didn't take long before I decided to go fully electric. Price to performance gave a clear green light to the **Tesla Model Y RWD**. In September 2023 I sat behind its wheel for the first time. It was the **first brand-new car of my life** – until then I had only ever bought used ones.

I wish everyone could feel that first drive. Instant response, power, silence and comfort. All of that adds up to **a much calmer way of driving**.

Today the car has **over 60,000 km** on the clock. I won't review the car itself, but life with an EV as such. As for the car: I'd buy it again without hesitation.

## Charging at home is what matters most

If you live in a house or have a garage with power, an EV is made for you. **A fully charged, pre-heated (or pre-cooled) car every morning** is a benefit you only truly appreciate over time.

Home charging is the killer feature. Priceless, whatever electricity costs.

:::me How I do it
I charge at home with a **3-phase SmartEVSE wallbox** (6–16 A, so up to ~11 kW). I charge it fully about **once a week**. The Tesla is in Home Assistant via ESP and Bluetooth, so the house knows how much is in the car's battery.

Charging has four modes: **Off / Now / From a time / AI** – in AI mode my integration [AI Energy](/en/projects/ai-energy/) picks the cheapest time based on spot prices. The solar inverter and the wallbox share one supply cable, so I keep the draw under **12 kW**. What I have on the roof and in the battery is in [My solar setup](/en/energy/my-solar/).
:::

I'm not saying an EV makes no sense if you live in a flat. But public charging isn't exactly fun. Just look at EV owners' groups – who is blocking chargers and how, which chargers are down right now. Not to mention what that kind of charging costs.

## Range doesn't matter. Consumption and charging speed do

At least for us. Sitting in the car for 6 hours without a break? No thanks. I never did that with a petrol car either.

So when I stop for a quick toilet break every couple of hours, I want the car to **charge in those few minutes**. Since I bought it, my Tesla has averaged **16.9 kWh/100 km** – summer and winter, town and motorway combined. When choosing an EV, look mainly at **how much it uses** and **how fast it charges** – not the range figure in the brochure.

## Rear-wheel drive? My fear was unfounded

This was what worried me most. I had only ever driven front-wheel-drive cars and associated rear-wheel drive with old BMWs or Ladas that were written off after the first snow.

The opposite turned out to be true. So far I **haven't had a single problem** – and where front-wheel-drive cars got stuck, I kept going.

## Winter eats range

Yes, winter is a range killer. Expect that if you do **300 km on the motorway in summer**, in winter it'll be **a bit over 200 km**. That's still a weakness of EVs which – I believe – progress will solve.

## Public charging – a card game

Charging on public chargers means **registration, an app or the provider's card**. And since there are many providers, you end up with a whole deck of cards. 🙂

And then the waiting. It's rare, but it happens and it's not a nice feeling. On our way back from Croatia we arrived with a flat battery at a charging park where **only 6 of 18 chargers worked** and there were **6 cars queuing** ahead of us. In the end we waited just under half an hour.

**Infrastructure will be key** to making the switch to EVs manageable at scale.

## What about the environment?

EVs are zero-emission – with one footnote: **locally zero-emission**. Otherwise, in my view, it's six of one and half a dozen of the other.

But if I had to choose between being locked in a garage with a running EV or a petrol car, the choice is obvious. Even though I like the smell of petrol, I pick the EV. 🙂

So yes – for a city where people live, EVs make **a big difference to air quality**. Globally, they're not that much of a miracle.

## Driving: try it yourself

If you've never driven an EV, try one yourself. Today almost any EV gives you a driving experience that beats an ordinary petrol car. **Instant response, acceleration, one-pedal driving and the silence** are magic.

:::tip What is one-pedal driving
When you lift off the accelerator, the car brakes using the motor and sends the energy back into the battery (regeneration). In town you barely need the brake.
:::

If someone says they miss the engine sound – then I don't understand why premium combustion cars try so hard to suppress it.

## What would make me go back to petrol?

I've thought about it a lot. Apart from being given such a car? Maybe money, if I couldn't afford an EV. And then infrastructure failing so you couldn't charge on the road.

But that can happen with petrol too…

## Summary

1. **With home charging**, an EV is simply a better everyday car.
2. **Don't worry about range** – look at consumption and charging speed.
3. **In winter**, expect roughly a third less range.
4. **Public charging** works, but be ready for apps, cards and the occasional wait.
5. **Rear-wheel drive** is no problem in winter.
